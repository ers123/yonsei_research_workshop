# From Parental Control to Self-Regulation: A Design-Led Intervention Linking Children's Screen Time and Physical Activity

Draft status: journal-aspiring internal draft v0.2 for DSB Lab review and the 2026-06-10 graduate-student workshop. This manuscript is not approved for external circulation, does not contain approved participant quotations, and does not claim publication readiness.

## Abstract

Children's smartphone use is often managed through parental restriction, monitoring, or conflict after excessive use has already occurred. This manuscript reframes the problem as a design question: how can an interface make the relationship between screen time and physical activity visible enough for children to interpret, plan, and regulate their own behavior? Building on prior DSB Lab pilot work on design-led intervention for children's smartphone use and physical activity (Kim & Shin, 2024; Kim et al., 2025), the study examines a screen-time and physical-activity-linked visual bar as a behavioral signifier. The proposed contribution is not that the intervention has already proven clinical or universal effectiveness. Rather, the manuscript argues that a persistent visual signifier may make behavioral consequences legible, thereby supporting perceived behavioral control, parent-child negotiation, and early forms of self-regulatory planning.

The present draft is based on a supplied corpus that includes pilot publications, app and design documentation, interview summaries, survey resources, and a merged screen-time/activity dataset. The quantitative dataset contains 2,341 rows from 19 participants across 2024-06-25 to 2025-01-16, but zero values, missing values, not-collected records, intervention timing, and grouping decisions still require formal cleaning rules before inferential claims can be made. Qualitative materials support provisional themes around planning, perceived control, family negotiation, feedback/feedforward, incentives, penalties, and exercise instrumentalization. The manuscript therefore positions the intervention as a promising design case for digital wellbeing and child-computer interaction while foregrounding the ethical and motivational limits of linking physical activity to screen-time access.

Keywords: screen time; physical activity; self-regulation; perceived behavioral control; child-computer interaction; design-led intervention; digital wellbeing

## 1. Introduction

Children's smartphone use has become a persistent concern across families, schools, public health discourse, and digital wellbeing research. In everyday household settings, screen-time management is often organized around restriction: parents set limits, children comply or resist, and conflict emerges when the rule is experienced as external control. This mode of regulation can make the parent the primary regulator and the child the target of regulation. It can also locate intervention after problematic use has already occurred, for example when a parent must verbally enforce a time limit or respond to conflict around device use.

The DSB Lab materials suggest a different design opportunity. Rather than asking only how parents can reduce children's screen time, this study asks how an interface can make the tradeoff between screen time and physical activity visible to children during use. In this framing, screen-time management is not only a household rule problem. It is also a problem of legibility. If children cannot see the consequences of their actions, rules may remain abstract, delayed, or externally imposed. If the system makes those consequences visible, children may have a better opportunity to interpret the rule, plan their next action, and participate in regulating their own behavior.

The intervention examined in this draft links smartphone use to physical activity through a persistent visual bar, extending the design intervention system proposed and explored in prior DSB Lab work (Kim & Shin, 2024; Kim et al., 2025). The bar indicates screen-time availability and activity requirements, allowing children to perceive the state of the rule before and during interaction. The central research question is: How does a screen-time and physical-activity-linked visual bar interface shape children's perceived behavioral control and self-regulation of smartphone use? Three secondary questions follow. First, after appropriate data cleaning, does the intervention show evidence of reduced screen time or increased physical activity? Second, which design elements appear to support planning, feedback interpretation, incentive response, or parent-child negotiation? Third, under what conditions does the intervention remain a form of external control rather than becoming a scaffold for internalized self-regulation?

The manuscript makes three intended contributions. First, it frames screen-time management as a design problem of visible behavioral consequence rather than only as a parental enforcement problem, building on the intervention lineage established in prior design work (Kim & Shin, 2024; Kim et al., 2025). Second, it connects a concrete interface element, the visual bar, to perceived behavioral control and self-regulatory planning (Ajzen, 1985). Third, it identifies a central design tension: linking exercise to screen-time access may support short-term behavior change while also risking the instrumentalization of physical activity. This tension is not treated as a flaw to be hidden, but as a theoretical boundary that should shape the interpretation of the intervention.

At this stage, the manuscript deliberately avoids claims that the evidence cannot yet support. It does not claim clinical efficacy, universal effectiveness, durable habit formation, or statistically validated causal effects. Instead, it develops a journal-aspiring argument that can be strengthened once data cleaning, quotation approval, translation review, and page-level evidence mapping are complete.

## 2. Theoretical Background

The theory of planned behavior provides a useful lens for interpreting the intervention because it foregrounds the relationship among intention, perceived control, and action (Ajzen, 1985). Behavior is not shaped only by instruction or external restriction. It is also shaped by whether a person perceives an action as possible, manageable, and connected to an intended outcome. Applied to children's screen-time regulation, perceived behavioral control helps explain why a visible interface might matter. If a child experiences a screen-time rule only as a parental command, behavior may depend mainly on monitoring, compliance, or resistance. If the rule becomes visible in a form the child can interpret, the design may support a different mechanism: the child can see what is possible, anticipate consequences, and plan the next action.

This theoretical framing must remain cautious. Although perceived behavioral control is central to the planned behavior framework (Ajzen, 1985), the current evidence does not yet prove that it improved in a validated psychological sense. The stronger defensible claim is that the visual bar is designed to support perceived control by making the activity-screen-time relationship legible. This distinction matters because design intent and qualitative interpretation can support an argument about mechanism, but they do not replace validated measurement.

Prior DSB Lab work provides the design-research foundation for the current manuscript. The conference paper proposed a design intervention system to regulate smartphone use and promote physical activity among children and adolescents (Kim & Shin, 2024). The subsequent pilot article explored the effects of a design-led intervention combining feedback, feedforward, incentives, and penalties in a home-use setting (Kim et al., 2025). These studies are important because they establish that the current intervention did not emerge as an isolated artifact. It developed from a research trajectory concerned with behavior-change design, child-computer interaction, digital wellbeing, and physical activity.

The current manuscript should nonetheless distinguish prior design work from current empirical evidence. The conference and pilot publications can motivate the intervention and clarify its design lineage (Kim & Shin, 2024; Kim et al., 2025), but they cannot substitute for evidence from the present corpus. Similarly, app and patent-related documents can describe system design, but they cannot prove behavioral effect. Interview summaries can support interpretation, but they require anonymization and quotation approval before they can become publication-ready evidence. Quantitative logs can show patterns, but only after documented cleaning and statistical decisions. A journal-level manuscript must keep these evidentiary roles separate.

The distinction among feedback, feedforward, incentives, and penalties is also theoretically important because these elements were central to the prior design-led intervention framing (Kim et al., 2025). Feedback helps users understand what has happened. Feedforward helps users anticipate what will happen if they act. Incentives and penalties structure consequences. A design that combines these elements may support planning, short-term behavior control, negotiation, reward seeking, or compliance in different ways. The manuscript's contribution depends on preserving these differences rather than treating the intervention as a single mechanism that simply "works" or "does not work."

## 3. Design Intervention

The intervention combines a persistent visual bar, a linkage between physical activity and available screen time, feedback and feedforward cues, incentive and penalty structures, and dashboard or data-flow components that make activity and use visible (Kim & Shin, 2024; Kim et al., 2025). For the present argument, the visual bar is the focal design element because it changes the timing and visibility of the rule. Instead of receiving only a parental command or retrospective correction, the child can see the behavioral condition while deciding what to do next.

The bar can be understood as a signifier because it indicates what action is possible, constrained, or required. It does not merely display status. It frames the relationship between action and consequence. When a child sees that more physical activity may change screen-time availability, the system turns an abstract household rule into an interpretable interaction state. In this sense, the design shifts the screen-time limit from a parent-only verbal instruction to a visible rule embedded in the child's device experience.

This shift matters because self-regulation should not be assumed to pre-exist the intervention. The visual bar does not make the child autonomous by itself. Rather, it may create conditions under which the child can notice, interpret, and plan around a behavioral constraint, a mechanism that aligns with the emphasis on planned action and perceived control in Ajzen's framework (Ajzen, 1985). A design intervention can therefore be evaluated not only by whether it produces immediate behavior change, but also by whether it changes how children and parents understand the rule.

The intervention also contains an unavoidable design tension. Incentives and penalties can make consequences clear, but clarity does not automatically equal autonomy. A child may become more active because the system makes activity useful for gaining screen time, not because physical activity has become intrinsically meaningful. This possibility is central to the manuscript. The design should be analyzed as a hybrid system: one that may support planning and perceived control while also relying partly on externally regulating mechanisms.

The corpus includes a researcher drafting brief, pilot and conference papers, presentation materials, app technology and patent-related documents, survey resources, interview summaries and transcripts, participant-level activity files, and a merged quantitative dataset. This range of materials is appropriate for a mixed-methods design-research article because it can connect design intent, intervention mechanism, observed interpretation, and behavioral data.

The current manuscript follows a staged synthesis approach. First, the source corpus was reviewed to identify the research question, intervention scope, available evidence, privacy boundaries, and claims that should not be made. Second, claims were mapped to source types and provisional findings. Third, unsupported claims, translation-sensitive concepts, privacy risks, and statistical claims requiring human approval were separated from the manuscript body. Fourth, the manuscript was drafted only from bounded claims that can be traced to the sanitized evidence map.

The qualitative materials appear to include interview summaries and transcripts across participant families and multiple rounds. These materials support provisional themes around planning, parent-child negotiation, reward and penalty interpretation, and exercise instrumentalization. However, this manuscript does not include direct quotations. Raw interview material must remain local until participant identifiers are removed, consent or IRB status is confirmed, quotation permissions are verified, and Korean-to-English translation choices are reviewed by a bilingual human reviewer.

The file appears to include screen-time and step-related variables, participant identifiers, dates, collection status, and intervention-date information. These facts are sufficient to describe the data resource, but not to justify causal or inferential claims. ## 5. Provisional Findings

### 5.1 Quantitative Readiness

The quantitative data are analysis-ready in principle, but not yet results-ready. The merged file provides a concrete basis for examining the relationship between screen time, physical activity, and intervention timing. It can support pre/post exploration and participant-level trajectory analysis once cleaning rules are approved. At this stage, however, the manuscript should not report p-values, effect sizes, subgroup differences, or causal effects.

A cautious quantitative section can state that the dataset enables analysis of screen-time and activity patterns across a multi-month collection period. It can also state that preliminary directionality should be treated as a working signal rather than proof. A working signal invites further analysis; a proof claim would require cleaned data, documented statistical procedures, and human approval.

The eventual quantitative analysis should answer a practical manuscript question: do behavioral patterns align with the proposed qualitative mechanism? If activity increases while motivation remains primarily external, the finding would support a nuanced interpretation rather than a simple success story. If screen time decreases only for some participants or under certain household conditions, the manuscript should identify boundary conditions rather than overstating general effectiveness. If missing or not-collected values change the interpretation, the final article should report that limitation directly.

### 5.2 Qualitative Interpretation

The qualitative materials support three provisional findings. First, the visual bar appears to make screen-time consequences more legible. Children may be better able to perceive remaining time, activity requirements, and the relation between current behavior and later smartphone availability. This supports the design claim that legibility matters. The bar gives children something to inspect and respond to, making planning more concrete than verbal instruction alone.

Second, parent-child negotiation may shift when children can see and manage the rule themselves. The supplied notes suggest that some parents experienced less direct verbal control when the interface made constraints visible. This should be phrased cautiously because the current draft does not yet include approved quotations or a formal qualitative coding table. Still, the theme is important. The intervention may change not only the child's individual behavior, but also the family interaction through which screen-time rules are discussed and enforced.

Third, feedback, feedforward, incentives, and penalties should not be treated as interchangeable. Feedback and feedforward appear more closely related to planning and interpretation. Incentives and penalties appear more closely related to short-term behavior control. This distinction is central because a behavior-change interface can support self-regulation and still contain mechanisms that remain externally regulating.

### 5.3 Boundary Conditions

The most important boundary condition is exercise instrumentalization. If physical activity becomes primarily a means of earning screen time, the intervention may increase activity without producing internalized exercise motivation. This is not a minor limitation. It is a central theoretical issue for any design that links a desirable behavior to digital reward. The intervention may help children plan and comply, but compliance should not be mistaken for self-regulation unless the evidence supports that stronger interpretation.

Other boundary conditions include baseline activity level, prior exercise motivation, family rules, technical reliability, and the child's interpretation of rewards and penalties. These factors may shape whether the bar is experienced as helpful information, a game-like challenge, a parental enforcement tool, or an external constraint. A submission-ready manuscript should make these conditions explicit because they help specify where the design is likely to work, fail, or require adaptation.

## 6. Discussion

The central contribution of this manuscript is the claim that screen-time management can be designed as a problem of visible consequences, not only as a problem of parental enforcement. The visual bar may support self-regulation by making the rule interpretable, extending prior design-led intervention work toward a more explicit account of perceived behavioral control (Ajzen, 1985; Kim et al., 2025). When children can see the relationship between physical activity and screen-time availability, they may be better able to plan use, anticipate consequences, and negotiate with parents. This interpretation connects interface design to behavioral theory without claiming that the intervention has already produced psychological transformation.

The distinction between self-regulation and compliance is crucial. A child may follow the rule because the interface makes the consequence visible, because the child wants more screen time, because a parent expects compliance, or because the child has begun to internalize a planning habit. These are different mechanisms. If the manuscript collapses all behavior change into "the intervention worked," it will lose the more interesting design-research argument. A stronger contribution is to show how visible consequence design can scaffold planning while also revealing the limits of reward-based behavior change.

The design also illustrates the ambivalence of incentives. Incentives can make action more immediate and understandable, especially for children. They can provide a concrete bridge between physical activity and digital access. Yet they may also narrow the meaning of exercise by making it instrumental to screen-time gain. The manuscript should not treat this as a failure of the design, but as a boundary of the mechanism. The intervention may be strongest when described as a scaffold for legible planning and family negotiation, not as a guarantee of intrinsic motivation.

This interpretation has implications for child-computer interaction and digital wellbeing. Many digital wellbeing tools focus on restriction, blocking, or retrospective reporting. The visual bar suggests another approach, consistent with the prior DSB design intervention trajectory: designing interfaces that make tradeoffs visible at the moment when users can still act (Kim & Shin, 2024; Kim et al., 2025). For children, this may be especially important because self-regulation develops through repeated opportunities to perceive, decide, and adjust. A well-designed signifier may not replace parental guidance, but it may change the form of that guidance by giving children a visible object around which negotiation can occur.

The manuscript also contributes a methodological caution for AI-assisted drafting in research contexts. A drafting system can help organize mixed materials, but it should not erase differences among evidence types. Design documents explain intent. Interviews explain interpretation. Logs show behavioral patterns. Theory frames mechanisms. None of these sources should be asked to do all of the work. The strength of the eventual article will depend on how clearly it assigns each source to the claim it can support.

## 7. Limitations And Next Analysis

This draft has four major limitations. First, the evidence map is not yet page-level. External circulation requires page references, excerpts, or approved analysis notes for the pilot publications, design documents, and theory sources. Second, the qualitative evidence is not yet quotation-ready. Raw Korean interviews and participant materials require anonymization, consent or IRB confirmation, and translation review before quotations can be used. Third, the quantitative analysis is not finalized. The team needs to confirm data cleaning, baseline and intervention windows, missingness, participant inclusion, and statistical tests. Fourth, translation-sensitive concepts need review. Terms such as "bar," "signifier," "perceived behavioral control," "self-regulation," "feedforward," "penalty," and "instrumentalization" will shape how the article is read.

The next analysis pass should produce three concrete artifacts: a privacy-safe quotation table, a data-cleaning and statistical-analysis memo, and an upgraded evidence map with page-level or excerpt-level support. Once these artifacts exist, the manuscript can move from journal-aspiring internal draft to serious submission-oriented revision.

## 8. Conclusion

The DSB Lab materials support a promising manuscript direction. The article should argue that a screen-time and physical-activity-linked visual bar can make behavioral consequences legible and may support planning, perceived control, and family negotiation (Ajzen, 1985; Kim et al., 2025). The strongest contribution is not the claim that the intervention simply reduces screen time. It is the design-research account of how visible consequence interfaces can shift screen-time management from parent-only restriction toward child-facing interpretation and self-management.

The manuscript should preserve its caution. Incentives and penalties may support short-term behavior but do not automatically produce internalized motivation. Physical activity may become instrumentalized. Quantitative patterns require cleaning and statistical review. Qualitative themes require anonymized evidence and translation review. These limits should be treated as part of the article's intellectual structure, not as afterthoughts.

As a journal-aspiring draft, the manuscript gives the research team a coherent argument to evaluate. It identifies the likely contribution, separates provisional findings from unsupported claims, and clarifies what must be strengthened before peer review. The next task is to decide which claims the DSB Lab wants to own, which evidence can support those claims, and which analysis steps are required before the manuscript can become submission-ready.
